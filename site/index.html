Our relationship with software is not right

Tools are extensions of ourselves. A hammer extends the arm; software extends the mind — our memory, attention, and sensemaking. That makes software intimate in a way few technologies are. And ours has been shaped by interests that are not our own.

Broken

Our data, workflows, and identities are trapped in app silos that refuse to interoperate. Working together across tools carries so much friction that collaborative sensemaking — the thing we most need right now — mostly doesn't happen.

Inadequate

One-size-fits-all tools ignore that we each think differently. Worse, most systems don't just store information — they freeze an interpretation and quietly enforce it forever. The tool stops being a partner in thinking and becomes a governor on thinking.

Controlled

Closed platforms mine our attention, weaponize our cognitive limitations against us, and hold our own work hostage — leaving us without genuine consent, autonomy, or ownership over our digital lives.

It's not the fruit that is the problem — it's the tree the fruit is growing from.

New apps grown in the same soil — extractive incentives, engagement metrics, growth at any cost — will keep bearing the same fruit. The foundation itself has to change: technology, at its base, is not yet relational. Making it so is what Seption is about.

Loyal software, worn by you alone

A prosthetic is deeply personal: fitted to the exact shape of the person it serves, and — once it fits — treated as part of them. Software should be a cognitive prosthetic in exactly that sense: an extension of your capability, not an external force shaping your behavior for someone else's benefit.

I am not comfortable putting on the prosthetics that Facebook or Google offer, because they are currently being worn by Facebook and Google.

We call the alternative loyal software: software with a fiduciary duty to its user. No dark patterns, no engagement loops, no lock-in — aligned incentives, transparency, and full ownership of your data and your tools.

The deeper aim is right relationship — a principle we learned from Indigenous wisdom traditions: technology rooted in mutual respect, ethical reciprocity, and harmony, honoring our interconnectedness with people, communities, and the natural world, and ensuring technology serves our well-being rather than extracting from it.

The grand vision: relational software that helps local communities become more self-sufficient and resilient, connected in a globally cooperative mutual-support network — sharing knowledge, workflows, and designs that each community can adapt to its own local context.

Our compass

The values that give this project direction. We hold them as commitments, not slogans.

Free, libre, open

Open source, open standards, no gatekeepers. Free as in freedom.

Design for emergence

Keep the core small — micro-standards a rich ecosystem can grow on top of, rather than features designed in from afar.

Meet people where they are

Organized the way people actually think — no ontology degree required.

Transcend and include

Build on the shoulders of hypertext, the semantic web, and the open web — extend them rather than discard them.

Mirror nature

Permaculture for the web: decentralized, adaptive, regenerative — flowing with complexity instead of fighting it.

Disagreement is first-class

Different communities can use the same terms differently; the system preserves disagreement rather than picking a winner. Interoperability without forced consensus.

Additive reinterpretation

Changing your mind never requires erasing past sensemaking. New interpretations layer on top of old ones — structure as a record of interpretation, not a verdict.

Care for and share the sources

Provenance, attribution, and consent built in — reciprocity toward the people and knowledge everything comes from.

What we're building

The research: a decentralized semantic hypergraph

Underneath everything is one representational idea: a graph with only one kind of thing in it. Every node can act as a relationship, and every relationship is itself a node — so relationships can carry their own context: provenance, confidence, time, source. Identity is content-addressed, the graph is local-first, and no single machine ever needs to hold the whole thing. Edit history is append-only, which is what makes additive reinterpretation possible at the technical level, not just as a promise.

Getting this representation right — without infinite regress or special cases — is a hard, genuinely open problem. It's the core of our research, and we work on it in the open.

Working today: a local-first RDF editor

Our first prototype is a browser-based editor for RDF 1.2 knowledge graphs — the laboratory where we test candidate representations by actually living in them. It already supports the full RDF 1.2 model (including triple terms and named graphs), multiple linked views of the same data — a relational tree, a node-link graph, a reader view, and SPARQL — a built-in library of ~900 standard vocabularies, and direct peer-to-peer sharing between machines with no central server. Everything lives on your device.

To be clear: this is a research prototype, not a product. We'd rather show you where we honestly are than overpromise — and where we are is far enough to be worth a conversation.
