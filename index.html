Why · The Problem

Our relationship with technology is not right.

Human brains are incredible, but we did not evolve for this. We have real cognitive limitations — attention, working memory, vulnerability to bias, difficulty with complexity — and we now live inside a relentless flood of information that presses on every one of them. Tools are extensions of ourselves; we need information tools equal to this world. The ones we have are not.

And it is worse than inadequacy. Under the guise of convenience and connectivity, Big Tech colonizes our lives — cloaking its hidden infrastructure with misleading jargon like “cloud” and “community,” quietly manufacturing our consent, monetizing our every move, and consolidating power in the name of “progress.” The powers that be weaponize our cognitive limitations with tools that are loyal to them. As George Carlin put it: “It's a big club, and you ain't in it.”

Our shared digital environments show the damage. They are polluted with digital junkfood and informational microplastics. They are one-size-fits-all, though none of us are. They fragment our knowledge across siloed apps and walled gardens — endless ephemeral flows, almost no durable stocks. And they leave us without genuine agency over our own data, identities, workflows, and communities.

You cannot fix problems with the same thinking that created them. It's not the fruit that is the problem — it's the tree the fruit grows from. Tools that keep growing in the same extractive soil will keep failing us, no matter how polished they get.

Why · A Different Soil

We are doing something different.

Simply put, the problem is that we are not in right relationship with all sorts of things — ourselves, each other, our software, our data, our communities, our biosphere. We are trying to solve that: first by finding right relationship with our digital devices by creating software to facilitate that, and then by using that software as a tool to facilitate right relationship with everything else.

Drawing on Indigenous wisdom and teachings, we've come to see that relationship is at the heart of it all. So we are designing for relationality above all else — in our licensing, our collaboration, our workflows, and the architecture of the data model itself. Two principles guide us: right relationship, technology rooted in mutual respect, reciprocity, and harmony; and natural law, systems that mirror nature's decentralized, self-organizing intelligence — emergence over enforcement, cooperation over control.

Loyal software

Software that doesn't weaponize your cognitive limitations against you. It serves you the way a fiduciary must — no hidden principal, no quiet redirection of your attention toward someone else's ends. That's right relationship.

Cognitive prosthetics

Tools extend our capacities; software tools extend our cognition. Like any prosthetic, they must be deeply personal — fitted to how you actually think — and we need autonomy over them in the same sacred way we have autonomy over our bodies, because they are extensions of ourselves.

What · Seption

A relational computing ecosystem.

Imagine mixing Vannevar Bush's Memex, Ted Nelson's Xanadu, and Tim Berners-Lee's Semantic Web, then adding radical interoperability with local files and the open web: a holonic hypermedia ecosystem built on a decentralized semantic hypergraph, with a loyal client for navigating, collaborating, and creating inside it — aligned with the principles of the DWeb.

Relational

Everything is a node — and every relationship is a node too, with its own context, provenance, and history. Meaning lives in the connections, the way it does in the world.

Computing

Local-first and decentralized: your graph lives with you, merges with others without a central server, and stays yours. The global graph is simply the union of everyone's.

Ecosystem

Not a product, not a platform — a protocol and a commons, built so that many tools, communities, and ways of thinking can grow on shared foundations.

Concretely, we are building what RDF should have had all along — edit history, n-ary relationality, permissions — as an RDF-compatible substrate where nodes are computed from immutable, content-addressed entries, so two peers who have seen the same entries agree without any coordination. Disagreement is first-class data: interoperability comes from shared accountability, not forced shared meaning. The technical thesis, in depth →

We're not building a product — yet. We're building a foundation of interoperable microstandards on which products can be built, one level below where everyone else starts.

The most powerful results often come from constraining ambition and designing only micro-standards on top of which a rich exploration of applications and concepts can be supported.

— Vint Cerf

How · The Practice

Small, honest experiments — in the open.

We work through design interventions: small prototypes, each scoped to test one specific thesis about how Seption should work. We build the simplest form that can prove or disprove the idea, share what we learn with our communities, and fold the learning forward — an Alexandrian process of shaping a living whole, not a waterfall toward a spec.

The current intervention is a working prototype: the Seption RDF editor, a local-first research instrument for building and navigating knowledge graphs — tree, graph, and reader lenses on the same living structure, full RDF 1.2 support, everything stored on your machine. It's where we are testing the link representations that the whole ecosystem will rest on. See the work so far →

And the horizon we're walking toward: relational software that helps local communities become more self-sufficient and resilient — connected in a globally cooperative mutual-support network that shares knowledge, workflows, designs, and tools, each adaptable to its own local context. Knowledge networked globally; the material economy kept local; the means of making technology democratized.

This only works as a we.

No single team can build this alone — and we wouldn't want to. We're looking for collaborators, critics, aligned funders, and fellow travelers who feel that our tools should belong to us. We don't have all the answers. We are humble, motivated, and working on it.
